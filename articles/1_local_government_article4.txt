**Environmental Ward Introduces 40‑metre Seclusion Corridor for Riverbank**  
*New buffer aims to protect native species and improve water quality*

Port Augusta, 6 December 2025 – In a move that many eco‑advocates say will give the riverbank a much‑needed breathing space, the City of Port Augusta’s Environmental Ward unveiled plans to create a 40‑metre “seclusion corridor” along the eastern bank of the River Murray. The corridor, fully developed by the autumn, will be a vegetated buffer zone designed to separate recreational land‑use from the sensitive riparian ecosystem, an effort that local wildlife groups say could make a big difference for native birds, fish and other flora.

### “A quiet refuge for our river”

Council Environmental Officer, Sarah Hughes, explained the corridor’s purpose to the 90‑person audience that gathered at the Pump Station Community Hall for the announcement.

“We’ve got to give the riverbank its own quiet corner,” Hughes told the press. “The seclusion corridor will use native plantings—gum‑tree seedlings, river bottlebrush and some shoreline grass—to create a natural buffer around 40 metres. That buffer will reduce the human footprint, curb erosion and give native wildlife a private space to thrive.”

Hughes added that the very term “seclusion” is an intentional nod to the corridor’s role as a sanctuary for the animals that rely on the riverbank. “They’re acting as a corridor, not a barrier,” she emphasized. “They’ll ‘seclude’ the wildlife from the surf‑and‑sand spots where people picnic and cycle, which is especially important when we’re dealing with sensitive species such as the endangered Murray cod and the crab‑claw.”

### Local community takes note

The proposal has already attracted voices from both sides of the now‑well‑paddled riverbank trail. Treaty Council member Brendan Austin, who sits on the Planning and Environment Committee, praised the initiative.

“If we are serious about protecting our river, it’s a good step,” Austin said. “People will still enjoy the walk or the bike path, but the 40‑metre strip will keep the wildlife in their natural habitat and protect the water quality. That’s a win for the whole community.”

Others, however, worry that the new buffer will limit public access to the scenic riverbank. “I love walking at sunrise behind the park,” said 65‑year‑old resident, Hilda Sellers. “I’m a little disappointed the area will be off‑limits. But if it helps the fish that are so important to Port’s fishing tradition, maybe it’ll be worth it.”

Council spokesperson, Anna Peters, conceded that the proposal will shift a few popular picnic spots but insisted the overall arrangement would still permit public use. “We are opening a new lookout platform, and the pedestrian path will be widened to accommodate more foot traffic,” Peters said. “The corridor is a look‑ahead measure; it’s not cutting off the river.”

### Legal and environmental backdrop

The 40‑metre seclusion corridor sits within a broader compliance framework tied to the *Aquatic Ecosystems Protection Act 2014* and the *Murray‑Darling Basin Plan.* Under those regimes, councils must provide “buffer zones” that support “water‑quality monitoring” and “habitat connectivity.” The corridor will meet the Ministry’s requirement for a 30‑metre buffer and falls well below the maximum 50 metre safe zone prescribed for high‑risk activities.

“The Murray‑Darling Basin Plan has been demanding stronger riparian protection, and we’re happy to be ahead of the curve,” said BASA (Bureau of Aquatic Science and Assessment) Director, Dr. Alan Rees. “This corridor is planned to incorporate a riparian planting scheme that will out‑grow currently exposed concrete slopes. The resulting vegetation and some leaf litter will break down, enriching the soil and providing natural filtration for the riverine water.”

### Implementation and timeline

According to the Environmental Ward, the corridor’s planting schedule begins late October, with full establishment expected by late March 2026. It will coincidentally align with the time of year when local anglers most frequently harvest Murray cod, giving the fish a protected head start before the peak breeding season.

Council officials say community involvement will remain a priority. “We’re launching a volunteer planting program next week,” stated Hughes. “Universities are also coming on board with environmental science students—an opportunity for hands‑on learning and adding credibility to our environmental stewardship.”

### Final thoughts

The Environmental Ward’s initiative is seen by many as a bold step toward preserving the unique biodiversity that the River Murray supports in Port Augusta and beyond. Whether it will sprint past the eco‑inequality that sometimes mars urban conservation projects remains to be seen, but the calm, 40‑metre corridor promises to be a quiet haven for wildlife—perhaps just what the river has been craving in the heartbeat of a growing city.
